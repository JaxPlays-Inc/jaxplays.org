From the moment the trolley cars glide across the screens and the 1904 St. Louis streets roll past, [[theatre:Alhambra Theatre and Dining|Alhambra Theatre & Dining]]'s production of [[production:2026 meet me in st louis|*Meet Me in St. Louis*]] transforms the stage into an immersive portal to a bygone era.<!--more--> Styled with the warmth of a vintage postcard, the experience immediately invokes deep sentimentality - a stark contrast to a modern world of text communication and fleeting digital connections. It returns the audience to a time when community sits at the center, families live side by side and a single shared phone call marks a momentous occasion.

At its heart, this piece demonstrates how family architecture and physical space intertwine. The production invites viewers directly into the Smith family living room, where the construct of home comes alive through intimate, memorable beats: Katie trims the house with holiday flair, little Tootie sits on Esther's lap during a song, dinner is held strictly at 6:30 p.m., and the entire family animatedly chimes in on whether the freshly made ketchup is "too sweet" or "too sour." It feels less like watching a play and more like stepping inside a living memory.

This nostalgic atmosphere is brought to life by an extraordinary creative team. Scenic designer [[person:Dave Dionne]] and property master [[person:Patti Eyler]] construct a rich, grounded world, while projection designer [[person:Bryce Cofield]]'s clever integration of front projection on stage alongside dual screens on opposite sides of the audience breathes life into the surrounding St. Louis area - reminiscent of the awe-inspiring introduction of electric lights at the Louisiana Purchase Exposition. Meanwhile, the costume design by [[person:Camala Pitts]] and [[person:Dorinda Quiles]], paired with intricate wig design and [[person:Johnny Pettegrew]]'s lighting, anchors every moment in historic detail, as audio engineer [[person:Matthew Carr]] ensures every note resonates throughout the space.

Under the direction of [[person:Shain Stroff]], musical direction by [[person:Cathy Murphy Giddens]] and lively choreography by [[person:Curtis Reed]], the cast delivers heartfelt performances across the board. [[person:Sofia Lourdes Smith]] shines as Esther Smith, effortlessly stepping into the iconic role famously defined by [[w:Judy Garland]], with grace. Smith captures the yearning ache of young romance in a delicate rendering of "The Boy Next Door," before leading a show-stopping rendition of "The Trolley Song," where choreography, technology and live performance achieve a beautiful synergy, giving the iconic journey momentum. Later, her tender delivery of the classic "Have Yourself a Merry Little Christmas" anchors one of the evening's most unforgettable moments, while her duet with [[person:Johnny Flannagan]] on "You Are for Loving" lands with stunning precision.

[[person:Kelly Wolfe]] delivers a captivating performance as Rose Smith, skillfully blending the eldest sister's fiery temperament with comedic brilliance that grounds the family's emotional core. Wolfe joins Esther and Katie, played by [[person:Patti Eyler]], for a delightful performance of "A Touch of the Irish," bringing joy to the scene. Johnny Flannagan is exceptional as John Truitt, delivering a standout performance marked by charm and sincerity. The family dynamic sparkles across numerous musical highlights, including an adorable rendition of "Under the Bamboo Tree" featuring Tootie, played by [[person:Ivy Ayres]], Agnes, played by [[person:Lily Davidson]], and Esther, as well as the charming "Wasn't It Fun?" featuring Mr. Alonzo Smith, played by [[person:Clay Smith]], and Mrs. Anna Smith, played by [[person:Martha Wingfield]]. The remarkably cohesive principal cast is rounded out by [[person:Elliot Miller]] as Lon and [[person:Dan Embree]] as Frank Prophater. A superb ensemble supports the leads, featuring memorable performances from [[person:Korey Kristensen]] as Warren Sheffield and [[person:Sarah Dickey]] as Lucille Ballard. The ensemble shines brightly during "Skip to My Lou" and "The Banjo," executing Reed's high-energy choreography to bring the party scenes to life.

The musical foundation, led by musical director and conductor Cathy Murphy Giddens, provides a vital, beating engine for the entire production. Conducting the backstage band with a steady hand, Giddens leads the musical score through seamless transitions between grand production numbers and the show's most vulnerable vocal solos. The live St. Louis Band - featuring Giddens conducting, [[person:Stephen Mark Fennell]] on piano, [[person:Tim Pickering]] on drums, [[person:Dan Rubel]] on bass guitar, and [[person:Ken Calhoun]] on guitar and banjo - keeps the energy soaring with a blend of turn-of-the-century flair that elevates the storytelling at every turn.

All of these elements culminate in the lovely finale at the glowing Grand Basin, center stage of the World's Fair. The scene brings the overarching theme full circle: despite all the changes, uncertainties and passage of time, home is found in community.
