[[theatre:Players by the Sea]]'s world premiere of [[production:2026 I Love You Youre Perfect Now Sign Here|*I Love You, You're Perfect... Now Sign Here*]] is a delightful, lighthearted comedy with enough real ache beneath the jokes that the wedding chaos never loses sight of the people caught in it.<!--more-->

Written and directed by [[person:David Sacks]], *Now Sign Here* follows Hannah and Michael, an engaged couple whose already complicated wedding planning is interrupted by dueling prenuptial agreements, clashing parents, class anxiety, religious expectation and one more life-altering surprise that refuses to wait until after the reception. On paper, that could be a sitcom pileup. In performance, the play works best because the cast treats the complications as funny without treating the people as disposable.

The production's center is the lived-in chemistry between [[person:Sara Sohn]] as Hannah and [[person:Alec Hadden]] as Michael. Their relationship has the texture of two people who know how to tease each other, worry each other, comfort each other and occasionally wound each other before they quite realize what they have done. Everything in the play leans on that connection. If we do not believe in Hannah and Michael, the rest is just paperwork and parental noise, and Sohn and Hadden make sure we believe.

Sohn gives Hannah a grounded, open quality that keeps the role from becoming only the anxious bride caught between family and fiance. Hannah is funny because she is smart enough to hear the absurdity around her and still human enough to get pulled into it. Sohn lets us see the fatigue behind the jokes, especially as Hannah begins to understand that the argument is not really about a document. It is about whether her parents can trust her to build a life that does not look exactly like the one they imagined.

Hadden threads a tricky needle as Michael. He is driven and intelligent, a future doctor with his own ambitions, but Hadden never lets the character lose the imprint of his blue-collar Brooklyn family. Michael can be reasonable, defensive, tender and exasperated in quick succession, and Hadden finds the humor in those pivots without making him seem foolish. The performance lands because Michael's pride and decency come from the same place.

As Ruth, [[person:Amy Allen Farmer]] is glorious in full overbearing-mother mode. Ruth is written as the kind of parent who can turn every announcement into a referendum on her own life, and Farmer knows exactly how far to push her. The performance is big, sharp and frequently hilarious, but it is not empty volume. Farmer gives Ruth enough conviction that her panic makes emotional sense, even when her behavior is spectacularly unreasonable.

[[person:Mark Hayes]] has the less showy but essential task of playing Abe, Ruth's long-suffering husband. He is the classic subservient spouse to an overbearing partner, and Hayes uses that position well. Abe's attempts to smooth over the room rarely solve anything, but they add a comic rhythm of their own: a man trying to keep the peace while knowing the peace has already left the building.

[[person:Evan Gould]] provides one of the evening's most reliable comic interruptions as the Rabbi. His scenes cut through the escalating wedding stress with a wandering, gently chaotic energy, as though spiritual counsel and personal digression have become indistinguishable. Gould makes those detours feel playful rather than merely tangential.

The thread between Taylor and Rick earns its place, with [[person:Alyssa Billings]] and [[person:Mitchell Wohl]] folding it into the play's larger argument about commitment. Their characters antagonize each other beautifully. Billings brings Taylor a brisk, guarded confidence, while Wohl gives Rick a loose comic defensiveness that keeps bouncing off her sharper edges. Together, they turn flirtation into sparring and sparring into something more vulnerable, until their arc starts echoing Hannah and Michael's in miniature.

[[person:Claire Cimino]] and [[person:Jason Collins]] are almost too adorable as Fiona and Sean, Michael's parents. They feel like two peas in the same pod, united by affection, bluntness and the faint disbelief of people who have wandered into a much more expensive argument than the one they expected. Collins finds especially nice moments when Sean's union background rubs against Abe's business-world assumptions, while Cimino's Fiona holds her own when Ruth steps over the line. They round out the family collision without turning it into a simple rich-versus-working-class joke.

What Sacks understands as a playwright is that a prenup is only funny for so long as an object. The comedy comes from what it exposes. Money becomes a proxy for trust. Wedding planning becomes a proxy for parental control. A rehearsal dinner becomes a referendum on class, geography and family identity. Even the play's dream logic, with imagined conversations spinning out before reality catches up, captures the way engaged couples can rehearse disaster before anyone has actually said the wrong thing.

That structure gives the production its buoyancy. The play is light on its feet, but it is not weightless. Beneath the jokes about invitations, delis, lottery winnings and Jewish mothers is a sincere question: How do two people begin a marriage when everyone around them wants a signature, a concession or a guarantee? The answer, wisely, is not that love makes everything simple. The answer is that love gives people a reason to keep choosing each other after the room gets loud.

*I Love You, You're Perfect... Now Sign Here* is warm, funny and disarmingly sweet, with a cast that knows how to ride the play's broad comedy without losing sight of its bruised little heart. Players by the Sea has given this world premiere an affectionate, capable launch, and its best moments suggest a romantic comedy with more on its mind than whether anyone signs on the dotted line.
