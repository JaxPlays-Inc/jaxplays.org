Theatre, at its absolute best, is an act of high-stakes alchemy. When [[w:James Goldman]]'s written dialogue, sharp as a freshly honed dagger and dripping with irony, meets a production team firing on all cylinders, the stage comes alive in magnificent fashion. [[theatre:Theatre Jacksonville]]'s production of [[production:2026 The Lion in Winter|*The Lion in Winter*]] is precisely that: a masterclass in theatrical integration.<!--more-->

Under the thoughtful direction of [[person:Jeff Grove]], this production builds a world that is visually captivating, emotionally visceral and cohesive. Grove crafts a unified vision that balances the colossal weight of history with the sharp savagery of a dysfunctional royal family at Christmas.

To bring Goldman's Plantagenet dynasty to life, a production must solve a unique spatial puzzle: how to convincingly transform a single stage into a sprawling medieval fortress, complete with throne rooms, claustrophobic bedrooms and dank dungeons.

The scenic elements provide a brilliantly layered architecture that yields an impressive variety of acting spaces. But the interplay of design elevates this set into a living space. [[person:Johnny Pettegrew]]'s lighting design is nothing short of exquisite, managing the difficult tightrope between vast medieval expanse and intense personal intimacy. Consider the top of Act II, or those delicate, razor-sharp two-person scenes between Eleanor and Henry, or Eleanor alone at her mirror. Here, Pettegrew's light works in seamless tandem with Grove's staging. It draws the eye, highlights the life of the character and shifts the mood in an instant, expanding to show the cold scale of a drafty hall or tightening to create a claustrophobic pressure cooker for two lovers-turned-rivals.

Every design layer supports this delicate equilibrium. Classic Costumes delivers wardrobe that grounds these royals in physical weight and period texture, while [[person:Brent Mulberry]]'s sound design and dimensional music choices lend the production an atmospheric gravity. The dynamic physical storytelling is heightened by [[person:Ron Shreve]]'s impactful fight choreography, all presented with the slick visual framing of Green Onion Creatives' graphic design. Under the watchful eye of production manager [[person:Michelle Leigh]] and the exemplary execution of stage manager Katie Cress and assistant stage manager Margie Ricke, this mammoth two-hour, 30-minute production runs with absolute precision.

Still, all that visual magic is really an invitation, a canvas waiting for the actors to fill it with living breath. Goldman's script demands an ensemble capable of delivering high-stakes drama wrapped in witty banter, and this seven-person cast delivers in spades. Their movement, vocal dynamics and visual storytelling draw the audience into the political chessboard from the very first beat.

At the center of this storm are two absolute powerhouses: Rebeque Destro as Queen Eleanor of Aquitaine and [[person:Kenny Logsdon]] as King Henry II. To watch Destro and Logsdon command the stage is to witness a masterclass in acting chemistry. They clash like titans, trading Goldman's brilliant insults with effortless speed, yet both allow the audience to peek beneath the armor. Destro plays Eleanor with a brilliance and a vulnerable, bruised grace; her scene at the mirror alone is a master study in subtlety. Logsdon brings a roaring vitality to Henry, balancing the king's unyielding ego with the desperate, aging panic of a monarch who cannot bear to lose his legacy. Together, their dynamic is electrifying; you genuinely believe these two people could burn down Europe just to prove a point to one another.

Supporting this royal duel is an exceptionally talented ensemble:

[[person:Sydney Wissinger]] (Alais Capet): Wissinger is simply incredible. Armed with extraordinary vocal quality, striking poise and deep stage awareness, she turns a character who could easily be played as a mere pawn into a resonant, crucial heart of the narrative.

Dustin Ponder (Prince Richard): Ponder plays the formidable Richard the Lionheart with a grounded, brooding intensity. He carries the physical presence and martial weight the character demands while subtly revealing the wounded child beneath the warrior's exterior.

Eli Bruce Durham (Prince Geoffrey): Durham delivers a delightfully sharp performance as the calculating middle child. He inhabits Geoffrey with a quiet menace, making every tactical shift feel deeply dangerous.

[[person:Collin Galvin]] (Prince John): Galvin brings a dynamic energy to the spoiled youngest son, nailing the petulant beats required to make John the ultimate pawn in his parents' games.

Aiden Wood (King Philip II of France): Wood plays the young French king with slick confidence and a keen instinct for political maneuvering.

It is this total alignment across cast and creative team that elevates the production, leaving you eager to dive back into the history books and explore the rich, chaotic world of the Plantagenets for yourself. Theatre Jacksonville's *The Lion in Winter* is a triumph of collaborative storytelling, a production where direction, design and acting align to create an unforgettable night of theatre. Bravo to Jeff Grove and his extraordinary team. Do not miss this one!
