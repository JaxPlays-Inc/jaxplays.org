Twenty-five centuries after [[w:Sophocles]] first asked whether the law of the state could override the law of conscience, the question has not aged a day. [[theatre:Lumen Repertory Theatre]]'s world-premiere adaptation, [[production:2026 Antigone Unmasked|*Antigone: Unmasked*]], written and directed by [[person:Brian Niece]], takes that ancient argument and sets it down in a country close enough to recognize: an unnamed place crawling out of civil war, governed by a man who has mistaken order for justice, watched over by citizens who catch the decree on their screens between other things and keep scrolling.<!--more-->

That relocation is the production's central gambit, and it is a shrewd one. Sophocles wrote for a Thebes his audience understood as both myth and mirror. Niece removes the myth. There are no gods issuing decrees from offstage here, no comfortable distance of antiquity to hide behind. The war is recent. The bodies are recent. Polyneices is "Nick," Antigone is "Annie," Ismene is "Izzy" — the legendary pulled down into the domestic, which is exactly where this version wants to live. When Creon forbids the burial of one brother while honoring the other, the decree does not read as the whim of a mythic tyrant; it reads as the kind of order a frightened government issues when it has decided that the surest path to peace is to control who is allowed to be mourned. Antigone's insistence that her brother be buried becomes something sharper than family piety. It becomes an argument that the dead deserve a name, and that a state which can erase a name can erase anything.

The title is not decoration. Niece's great structural invention is the mask: the citizens, the apprentices, Creon himself all begin the play behind one, and much of the evening's drama is the drama of who removes theirs, and when. Antigone never wears one — she is unmasked from the first, which is her whole character in a single image. Haemon sets his down the moment he breaks from his father. Creon is the last masked figure standing, and the production holds his unmasking for the very end, staging it as neither punishment nor mercy but plain exposure. Sophocles actors already wore literal masks; Niece inverts the old convention and makes the mask mean something — the performance of certainty, the face you keep on so the machinery can keep running.

What makes the adaptation land is its refusal to make the argument easy. The strongest Antigone productions resist flattening Creon into a villain, and this one holds that line and then walks past it. Eugene Lindsey plays Creon as a powerhouse of conviction, a man bringing resolve to a world with no stable ground beneath it. His certainty is not cruelty; it is the terrible, familiar logic of a leader who believes that holding firm is the only thing standing between his people and another collapse. Niece writes him a scene Sophocles would not recognize but, I suspect, would admire: a private negotiation, alone with Antigone, in which Creon offers not to spare her but to *reclassify* her — to let Nick's burial stand as something the state chose to permit rather than a crime it must punish. "I'm asking you to be quiet about thinking you are right," he tells her. It is the most dangerous scene in the play precisely because the offer is genuine, and because for one long beat you can feel Antigone's whole life hanging inside his arithmetic. Her refusal — "You're offering me silence" — is the hinge the evening turns on.

Maggie Cox meets him with an Antigone of unwavering force. She is strong and convicted, never questioning, never bending, fighting for what she knows to be right. It is a performance built on certainty, and Cox never apologizes for it. But the production is wise enough to know certainty alone cannot carry a tragedy, so it surrounds her with doubt — and here *Antigone: Unmasked* makes its most thrilling departure from the source.

That doubt lives most movingly in Kate McManus, whose Ismene is the heart of the show. In Sophocles, Ismene is the cautious sister, the foil, the one who stays home and is barely heard from again. Niece hands her the production's quietly radical counter-argument: that staying is harder than going, that survival is its own moral act, that "a thousand small acts" of endurance might ask more courage than one magnificent refusal. McManus carries the full emotional weight of what Antigone's defiance costs — to her sister and to herself — and when she finally lowers her mask in a scene that has been building since the lights came up, it breaks the audience open precisely because the play will not tell us which sister is right. Noah Bennett's Haemon is the show's mind, its reasoning engine, a man caught between belief and consequence and weighing the price of every conviction. The question he presses on his father — *what if the law is wrong?* — is the one the production most wants its audience to sit inside.

Presiding over all of it is Zonnetta Marie as the Chorus, and she is breathtaking. Niece reconceives the Greek chorus as a kind of angelic watcher, a citizen who never quite looks away, and Marie inhabits that role with remarkable strength. Marie's Choris is always observing, often still, but never concrete, a presence more felt than fixed. Her voice is the production's great instrument, as gentle as a whisper in one moment and a tidal wave the next. But the role carries a sting Sophocles never built into it. This Chorus can witness and cannot intervene — "that is the specific tragedy of being the witness" — and near the end, having offered the whole truth to the one man who most needs to hear it and watched him keep his back turned, she says only, "We tried. We always try." In a story about a society that would rather its citizens avert their eyes, a Chorus that refuses to stop watching is not decoration. It is the argument and the indictment.

The staging makes that idea legible in space. Niece and the Lumen Rep team divide the action across three distinct playing areas: an elevated platform of official power, a threshold ramp where negotiation and witness happen, and a circle of truth and human consequence. The geography does real dramatic work. Power sits above, consequence gathers below.

Where the production is most alive is in what it declines to resolve. A lesser adaptation would tell you who was right. This one leaves the hardest questions in the audience's lap. Is Antigone a martyr or an absolutist? Is Ismene a coward or the braver one, the one who stays to do the slow work? Is Creon a tyrant or a steward doing the ugly arithmetic of survival? Even the ending withholds the old catharsis. Sophocles punishes Creon with a cascade of corpses; Niece leaves him alive, unmasked and comprehending, which is worse. Haemon's death is reframed not as rage but as fidelity — a choice to be "wherever she is." And the play closes not on a body but on a question thrown directly at the house: *What will we do now?* The citizens who watched it all scroll past on their screens are handed the question, and the lights fall before anyone can answer.

The evening also marks the debut of Lumen Rep's NEXTGEN Apprentice Company, which places five emerging artists — Dev Acharya, Jessica Coleman, Logan Everson, Kirstyn Lewis and Valeria Santiago — in the ensemble. Niece uses them pointedly. For most of the night they are the state's masked machinery; in the final movement, they are the ones who remove Creon's mask and close around him as "the consequence." Folding apprentices into a story about who inherits the world the powerful leave behind is a quietly fitting choice for a company staking out its own future.

*Antigone: Unmasked* is not a museum piece and does not want to be. It is a two-and-a-half-millennium-old warning, unmasked and handed back to us with the dust blown off, and Lumen Rep has the good sense to let it stay uncomfortable. The dead deserve a name. So, this production insists, do the living who refuse to look away.
