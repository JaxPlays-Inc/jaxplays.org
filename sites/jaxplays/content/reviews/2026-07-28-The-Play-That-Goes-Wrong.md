Everything goes wrong in [[theatre:Players by the Sea|Players by the Sea]]'s [[production:2026 The Play That Goes Wrong|*The Play That Goes Wrong*]], which is exactly why it goes so right.

<!--more-->

Henry Lewis, Jonathan Sayer and Henry Shields' farce follows a theatre troupe attempting to present a respectable 1920s murder mystery while the production slowly, then rapidly, then catastrophically betrays them. The pleasure of the show is not suspense over whether disaster will arrive. Disaster is the house style. The fun is watching how confidently everyone keeps marching toward ruin, as if dignity might still be available somewhere under the broken furniture.

This is a comedy about commitment, and director [[person:Tracy Anne Olin]]'s production understands that the cast has to play the stupidity with total seriousness. Nobody can wink too hard at the audience. Nobody can act as though they are above the joke. The characters must believe, with every doomed inch of themselves, that the play can still be saved. That shared conviction is what makes the evening so funny.

The result is a stage full of people behaving with spectacular confidence and spectacularly terrible judgment. [[person:Neal Thorburn]]'s Chris tries to hold the evening together through sheer force of theatrical authority. [[person:Anvar Gashimov]]'s Robert leans into the grand self-importance of an actor determined to make the moment bigger, even when the moment is already collapsing around him. [[person:Ashlyn Brown]]'s Sandra brings the sort of melodramatic poise that becomes funnier the less the production deserves it.

Around them, the foolishness keeps multiplying. [[person:Chris Watson]]'s Max turns eagerness itself into a comic condition, delighted by the audience and catastrophically unhelpful in almost equal measure. [[person:Jay Bilderback]]'s Dennis makes uncertainty visible in the funniest possible ways, while [[person:Jan Buksar]]'s Jonathan, [[person:Miles Genaovargas]]' Annie and [[person:Frankie Rady]]'s Trevor help keep the machinery of mayhem spinning. Everyone is spectacularly stupid in this, and I mean that as the highest compliment. Farce only works when the performers are smart enough to play bad choices cleanly, and this company clearly knows the assignment.

The design and backstage demands of *The Play That Goes Wrong* are no small joke either. A show built on malfunction has to be engineered with brutal precision, and the production's pleasure comes from the sense that chaos is being released on cue. [[person:Tom Cassaro]]'s scenic design and master carpentry, [[person:GoldenStage Productions]]' lighting and sound design, [[person:Heather Goliber]] and [[person:Katarina Goliber]]'s props, and the stage management team of [[person:Lora Christl]] and [[person:Jillian Cote]] all support that central illusion: that everything is falling apart like clockwork — especially when the clock doesn't work either.

There is not much need to over-explain an evening like this. It is built to be laughed at, loudly and often, and Players by the Sea delivers exactly that. *The Play That Goes Wrong* is wonderfully terrible, absolutely ridiculous and a reminder that sometimes the surest route to theatrical joy is watching everyone onstage make the worst possible decision with complete conviction.
