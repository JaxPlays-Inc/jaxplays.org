For its first production, [[theatre:Catalyst Theatre]] chose a show that does not tiptoe into the room. [[production:2026 The Great American Trailer Park Musical|*The Great American Trailer Park Musical*]] is loud, broad, shamelessly silly and built for an audience willing to laugh before it asks too many questions. Catalyst meets it on those terms, and the result is a fun, good-hearted debut with enough comic snap and musical confidence to make the evening land.<!--more-->

The musical takes place in Armadillo Acres, a fictitious trailer park set in the real city of Stark, Florida, where Pippi's arrival knocks Jeannie and Norbert Garstecki's already fragile marriage sideways. The plot has the shape of a sitcom collision: a restless husband, a housebound wife, a dangerous ex and neighbors who know everyone's business before anyone has finished making the mistake.

What makes Catalyst's production work is that director [[person:Sherrie Provence]] and the cast do not pretend the material is subtler than it is. They lean into the comedy without letting it curdle. The show stays light on its feet, and when the script offers a stereotype, the performers usually find a human behavior inside it: embarrassment, longing, panic, pride, jealousy, denial. That small layer of recognition keeps the production from becoming only a parade of punchlines.

The clearest comic engine is the trio of Betty, Lin and Pickles, played by [[person:Renee Riso]], [[person:Keri Green]] and [[person:Grace Lyndsey]]. They operate as a pseudo-Greek chorus for Armadillo Acres, narrating the chaos, commenting on the damage and often improving the joke simply by being nearby. The three are hilarious together, but the pleasure is how distinctly each of them lands.

Riso's Betty has the settled authority of a woman who has already survived every version of this nonsense. Green's Lin brings a sharper edge, cutting through the commotion with the calm of someone who refuses to be surprised. Lyndsey's Pickles is lighter and stranger, with a comic openness that keeps the trio from becoming too hard or too knowing. Each carries her fair share of the comedic weight, and together they give the production its rhythm. When they are in command, the show feels most sure of itself.

The central triangle also benefits from performers who keep reaching past the book's easy labels. [[person:Heather Starling]] gives Jeannie a recognizable, relatable frustration beneath the agoraphobia jokes. Her funniest moments are grounded in the sense that Jeannie has been stuck with her fear, her marriage and herself for too long, and the pressure is finally leaking out in odd directions.

[[person:Hunter Showalter]] makes Norbert more relatable than the role has any right to be. Norbert is written as the husband caught in the oldest bad-decision machine in comedy, but Showalter lets us see the weakness, embarrassment and need underneath the foolishness. He is funny without sanding off the character's flaws, which is exactly the balance the role needs.

[[person:Heather Quinn]] gives Pippi a similar lift. The script introduces her as disruption in human form, but Quinn plays more than the disruption. Her Pippi has confidence, warmth and a little weariness around the edges, enough to suggest a person who has been running long before she reaches this particular trailer park. Starling, Showalter and Quinn each find hilarious and relatable moments inside roles that could easily collapse into sitcom tropes.

As Duke, [[person:Xavier Torres]] pushes the plot's danger into the park with the right amount of excess. The role is intentionally outsized, and Torres gives the production a jolt when that outside chaos catches up with Pippi.

The production's musical life is one of its strongest assets. [[person:Keith Teepen]]'s hand is heard throughout in the ensemble sound, especially in the harmonies, which are fantastic. In a show this goofy, clean vocal work matters more than it might seem. The harmonies keep the comedy buoyant and give the score a polish that helps the production feel held together even when the story is racing toward another bad choice.

[[person:Jonathan Chapman]]'s choreography is mostly simple, but that simplicity feels right for the world of the show. The movement looks natural and normal for these characters, which is important. Armadillo Acres should not suddenly become sleek every time the music starts. Chapman lets the staging feel casual until it needs to become bigger, and when it tips into absurdity during the disco number "Storm's A-Brewin'," the shift feels like an earned comic escalation rather than a separate idea dropped into the show.

That is the production's best quality overall: it understands the difference between casual and careless. The comedy can be broad, but the cast still has to listen. The music can be ridiculous, but the harmonies still have to lock in. The choreography can look easy, but it still has to shape the joke. Catalyst's inaugural production succeeds because enough of those pieces are handled with care.

*The Great American Trailer Park Musical* is not an elegant show, and it is not trying to be. It is a messy little comedy with a book that often reaches for the easiest laugh. But Catalyst Theatre’s production is lively and genuinely funny, with bright ensemble harmonies and a cast that finds more warmth in the material than the material always provides. For a first outing, that is a promising start: a company stepping onto the local stage with energy, humor and a clear willingness to have a good time.
