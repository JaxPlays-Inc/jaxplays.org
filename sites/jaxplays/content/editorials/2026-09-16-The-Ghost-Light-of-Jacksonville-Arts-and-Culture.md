Walk into almost any theater after everyone has gone home and you may find it dark except for one thing.

Center stage, on a stand about shoulder height, a single bare bulb is burning.

The Ghost Light.

The practical explanation is simple: it keeps the first and last person through the door from walking off the edge of the stage into the orchestra pit.
<!--more-->
You leave the light on because somebody is coming back.

Because soon there will be a load-in. Sawdust on the shop floor. A stage manager with a binder. Actors calling lines from the wings. And a few hundred people sitting together in the dark watching something happen that will never happen exactly that way again.

The Ghost Light is preparation for what comes next.

Jacksonville's cultural sector is at a moment when that kind of preparation matters.

Over the past three fiscal years, Duval County arts and cultural organizations have absorbed $6.1 million in state funding cuts. Twenty-five of the Cultural Council's 32 Cultural Service Grant organizations experienced reduced state funding.

And more uncertainty is on the horizon at the city level.

In November, Florida voters will consider Amendment 3, a property tax measure that could change the revenue available to local governments, including Jacksonville. Ahead of the vote, City Council has made funding for many organizations contingent on the outcome of Amendment 3, including the Cultural Council.

Whatever voters decide, the larger lesson is one every organization and business understands: the environment you operate in will change.

Funding changes. Economies shift. Cities grow. Priorities evolve.

The question is whether you wait for change to arrive or prepare for it.

Jacksonville has a lot worth preparing for.

Nearly 2.1 million people attend events produced by Cultural Service Grant organizations each year. Duval County residents spend an average of approximately $87 per person, per event, beyond admission.

A show fills restaurant tables and hotel rooms. It creates demand for food and beverage, parking, transportation and retail. It brings people into public spaces and gives them a reason to stay longer, explore more and return.

For every performance audiences see, there is an economy working backstage.

More than 5,600 Jacksonville residents earn income through arts and culture. There are actors and musicians, certainly, but also carpenters, technicians, administrators, educators and designers whose names may never appear above the title.

Last year, the Cultural Service Grant Program generated $143.4 million in local economic activity. Across five years, every dollar the city invested through the program generated an average of approximately $29 in return.

And 91% of that economic impact was generated by organizations located downtown.

Those numbers matter because public investment deserves accountability. But they also tell us something bigger.

Jacksonville has built an asset worth strengthening.

More than 207,000 young people benefited from Cultural Service Grant organizations last year. Artists found work. Small businesses found customers. Families found places to spend an evening together. Audiences found reasons to explore Jacksonville and experience something new.

Arts and culture feed the heart of a city. And that heart helps feed its economy.

Our responsibility at the Cultural Council is to help make that ecosystem stronger.

Under Chapter 118, the Cultural Council serves as Jacksonville's designated local arts agency. For more than 50 years, we have helped steward public cultural investment and connect Jacksonville's creative community with resources and opportunities.

But regranting is only part of that work.

We connect artists with opportunities. Build relationships between the cultural and business communities. Champion for Jacksonville's creative economy. Create visibility for the people shaping our cultural identity. And help bring public art into the places Jacksonville residents experience every day.

Increasingly, that work is about future-proofing Jacksonville's cultural economy.

Future-proofing does not mean predicting every funding decision or economic shift ahead.

It means building enough strength in our sector so that we can be nimble enough to weather any storm.

That requires cross-sector partnerships, stronger connections to the business community, opportunities for artists and creative entrepreneurs, advocacy, visibility and people who understand that a vibrant cultural sector is part of a vibrant Jacksonville.

Public investment will remain an important part of that equation. But the future of Jacksonville's creative economy cannot rest on any single funding source.

It will take businesses, residents, civic leaders and cultural organizations working together to build something resilient enough to grow through change.

Supporting the Cultural Council of Greater Jacksonville helps us champion the sector, connect artists with opportunities, build partnerships, increase visibility and create a stronger cultural economy.

Because eventually, every Ghost Light is switched off.

The house opens. The audience arrives. Someone steps onto the stage.

The light was never there because everyone expected the theater to stay empty.

It was there because someone had prepared for what came next.

The creative sector should, too.

---

Diana Donovan is executive director and CEO of the Cultural Council of Greater Jacksonville, the city's designated local arts agency under Chapter 118.
