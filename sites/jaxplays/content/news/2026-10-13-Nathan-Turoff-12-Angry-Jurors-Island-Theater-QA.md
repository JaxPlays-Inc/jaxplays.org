This November, [[theatre:The Island Theater]] invites audiences into the jury room with [[production:2026 12 Angry Jurors|*12 Angry Jurors*]], an adaptation of Reginald Rose's *Twelve Angry Men*.<!--more-->

JaxPlays spoke with director Nathan Turoff about bringing the story to the stage, working with the cast and what to expect when the production opens.

*This interview has been edited for length and clarity.*

**JaxPlays: *12 Angry Jurors* is an adaptation of Reginald Rose's *Twelve Angry Men*. What drew you to this particular version of the play, and what do you think makes the story so enduring?**

**Nathan Turoff:** Both the movie and the play have been two of my favorites of each medium since I first saw and read them in middle and high school, respectively. To be given the opportunity to direct such a classic for my directing debut has been an utmost honor.

**JaxPlays: The title shifts from *Twelve Angry Men* to *Twelve Angry Jurors*. How does this adaptation broaden the story, and what opportunities does it create for the cast and creative team?**

**Turoff:** The story itself doesn't change too much. The show can be performed as *Twelve Angry Men*, if it's an all-male cast, *Twelve Angry Women* if it's an all-female cast, or *Twelve Angry Jurors* if it's a mix of both. As anyone who's done community theatre knows, men are usually much harder to come by. I personally chose to do *Twelve Angry Jurors* so I could be more flexible and fair with my casting. While in the 1950s seeing an all-male jury might have been commonplace, I just don't think that a modern day jury would be so homogeneous.

**JaxPlays: Much of the play takes place in a single jury room, yet the tension continues to build throughout the story. How are you approaching the pacing, staging and atmosphere to keep the audience engaged?**

**Turoff:** While the single-room staging can seem restrictive and claustrophobic from a directorial and audience standpoint, that's what honestly sells it. Twelve people cramped in a room, un-allowed to leave or do anything besides what they are there for. It would be easy to just have them all sitting in their seats for two hours like some stereotypical executive board meeting, but that's just not how most humans are these days. I am using all of my lighting options to my advantage to help mitigate any potential for audience fatigue. I come from an extensive family background in theatre, but especially in stage lighting.

**JaxPlays: The play depends on 12 distinct personalities, each bringing their own experiences, assumptions and perspectives into the room. How have you worked with the actors to develop such a strong ensemble while ensuring each juror has a distinct voice?**

**Turoff:** All these jurors are written to be relatable and realistic. I think the script does this very well, as I can see aspects of myself in each and every one of these characters. I love talking with my actors to help develop their characters, but a lot of it comes from amazing talent of the actors themselves. In my opinion, the director should be listening to his actors just as much as they listen to him. I've worked hard to create a strong bond between my cast. It was fairly easy to do this given it's only 12 people, but not without difficulties. This aforementioned strong bond has allowed me to encourage incredible amounts of creative freedom in my cast. I openly welcome feedback and opinions from my cast and crew about my decisions. I *want* to see them decide their own character choices. I always advocate for my cast to try things even if it may contradict what I've initially told them. I have an amazing sense of faith and confidence in my cast to make smart choices, and in the end, their interpretations of these characters will be all the better because of it.

**JaxPlays: At its heart, this story examines reasonable doubt, personal bias, group pressure and the responsibility that comes with making a life-altering decision. Which of these themes feels especially important to you in this production?**

**Turoff:** One of the show's most important themes is critical thinking. One of Juror No. 8's first poignant lines, and one of my personal favorites is: "There were eleven votes for 'guilty.' It's not easy for me to raise my hand and send a boy off to die without talking about it first." You should never rush decisions, *especially* when it's life or death. A deeper look, and especially a new perspective, are crucial to understanding and overcoming adversity and obstacles. Much of the case seems open and shut at first glance and even second glance. Throughout the story, Juror No. 8 shows them that there's more than meets the eye. In my opinion the evidence being used against the boy is incredibly circumstantial and full of inconsistencies. It says a lot that 11 of them, albeit some not intentionally, take all this evidence at first glance as foolproof, and try to send a boy to die. The phrase "Don't judge a book by its cover" may sound like what I'm getting at here, but I think a more appropriate phrase would be "Don't judge a book by its first chapter." You see it all the time in the modern era of the internet, social media and AI, where so many people are quick to make assumptions and rush judgements on things on which they know little. That quick rush of judgement is one of the notions this play challenges. It was relevant in 1957, and it is just as relevant now, if not more.

**JaxPlays: The audience watches the jurors debate the evidence and challenge one another's assumptions. How do you hope audiences will respond to the characters and reflect on their own beliefs as they watch the play unfold?**

**Turoff:** I'm hoping that theatregoers are able to identify and relate to these characters just like I have. This is a very grounded story with very grounded characters. I believe that as the audience learns more about the case, and thinks on it along with the jurors, they'll start to think about the "why?" as well.

**JaxPlays: With so much of the action driven by dialogue, conflict and shifting power dynamics, what have been some of the biggest challenges, or most rewarding discoveries, in bringing this production to life?**

**Turoff:** It seems like such a simple story in theory: "Twelve people decide if someone is guilty or not." Well, to quote the late Paul Newman, "I prefer simple plots with complex characters rather than complex plots with simple characters." The story truly is just 12 people in a room for the whole show. All of these characters are incredibly complex and multifaceted. This show is reliant almost entirely on the performances of its stars. It can only work with a strong set of talented performers to accurately convey these characters, and my cast are all that and more.

**JaxPlays: Is there a particular moment, exchange or character dynamic that you find especially compelling, perhaps one that has taken on new meaning for you during rehearsals?**

**Turoff:** I won't go into too much detail as I don't want to spoil too much. There is a point later in the play where one of the more "antagonistic" (but sadly not unrealistic in today's world) jurors says some very nasty things. Seeing how all the jurors react to things in different ways is one of the best parts of this show in my opinion, and it especially shines in this moment.

**JaxPlays: What do you hope audiences take away from *Twelve Angry Jurors* when they leave the theatre?**

**Turoff:** I believe that people will walk away from this show with a greater appreciation for abstract thought and critical thinking. We're hoping people can come to realize how dangerously quick humanity is to form strong opinions. The aim is to make the audience return from this show with a greater desire to ask "why?" I hope they can resume their lives ready to think about the reasons behind we do what we do. Human thought should never be taken for granted.

**JaxPlays: Finally, what would you say to someone considering attending the production who may already know *Twelve Angry Men*, or someone experiencing this story for the very first time?**

**Turoff:** I would say that this story is as timeless as it gets. Its themes and greater implications were topical then, and they're topical now. This show challenges how you examine the world around you, and the ways in which we all interact with each other. The difference in cast demographics compared to the all-male original makes it well worth the watch on its own. When adding on everything else, you'd be leaving this theatre with a greater sense of intelligence, understanding and patience.
