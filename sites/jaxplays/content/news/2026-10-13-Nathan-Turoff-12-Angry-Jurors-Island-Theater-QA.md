This November, [[theatre:The Island Theater]] invites audiences into the jury room with [[production:2026 12 Angry Jurors|*12 Angry Jurors*]], a compelling adaptation of Reginald Rose's classic *Twelve Angry Men*.<!--more-->

JaxPlays caught up with director Nathan Turoff to talk about bringing this timeless story to the stage, working with the cast and what audiences can look forward to when the production opens.

**JaxPlays: *12 Angry Jurors* is an adaptation of Reginald Rose's *Twelve Angry Men*. What drew you to this particular version of the play, and what do you think makes the story so enduring?**

**Nathan Turoff:** Both the movie and the play have been two of my favorites of each medium since I first saw and read them in middle and high school, respectively. This story has stood the test of time, and continues to be relevant to this day. To be given the opportunity to direct such a classic for my directing debut has been an utmost honor. It's been equally challenging and rewarding.

**JaxPlays: The title shifts from *Twelve Angry Men* to *Twelve Angry Jurors*. How does this adaptation broaden the story, and what opportunities does it create for the cast and creative team?**

**Turoff:** The story itself doesn't change too much. The show can be performed as *Twelve Angry Men*, if it's an all-male cast, *Twelve Angry Women* if it's an all-female cast, or *Twelve Angry Jurors* if it's a mix of both. As anyone who's done community theater knows, men are usually much harder to come by. I personally chose to do *Twelve Angry Jurors* so I could be more flexible and fair with my casting. It also helps to make it more realistic in my opinion. While in the 50's seeing an all-male jury might have been commonplace, I just don't think that a modern day jury would be so homogeneous.

**JaxPlays: Much of the play takes place in a single jury room, yet the tension continues to build throughout the story. How are you approaching the pacing, staging and atmosphere to keep the audience engaged?**

**Turoff:** While the single-room staging can seem restrictive and claustrophobic from a directorial and audience standpoint, that's what honestly sells it. Twelve people cramped in a room, un-allowed to leave or do anything besides what they are there for. As someone with ADHD who has trouble staying still and focused, that genuinely sounds awful. I'd probably be "Angry" too. It would be easy to just have them all sitting in their seats for 2 hours like some stereotypical executive board meeting, but that's just not how most humans are these days. It's also frankly boring from an audience perspective as well. I am using all of my lighting options to my advantage to help mitigate any potential for audience fatigue. I come from an extensive family background in theater, but especially in stage lighting. Lighting can affect the mood of a room just as much as background music can. In some versions of the script, as well as the 1957 movie, a thunderstorm rolls in, which helps set that ambiance. Normally this would be achieved primarily with stage lighting. While that element sadly isn't present in our script, it's a great example of light-based storytelling that I still find fun ways to utilize in this show.

**JaxPlays: The play depends on twelve distinct personalities, each bringing their own experiences, assumptions and perspectives into the room. How have you worked with the actors to develop such a strong ensemble while ensuring each juror has a distinct voice?**

**Turoff:** All these jurors are written to be relatable and realistic. I think the script does this very well, as I can see aspects of myself in each and every one of these characters. I love talking with my actors to help develop their characters, but a lot of it comes from amazing talent of the actors themselves. I've performed on stage dozens of times with dozens of different directors in my comparatively brief time in this business. While it's true that as director, it's my responsibility to drive the vision of this production, I could never dream to know everything, and some of my ideas may not be good ones and I just don't realize it. In my opinion the director should be listening to his actors just as much as they listen to him. I've worked hard to create a strong bond between my cast. It was fairly easy to do this given it's only twelve people, but not without difficulties. The main issue was not everyone being able to attend every rehearsal, but that's just part of the way it works. This aforementioned strong bond has allowed me to encourage incredible amounts of creative freedom in my cast. I openly welcome feedback and opinions from my cast and crew about my decisions. I also strongly encourage them to be creative themselves. I WANT to see them decide their own character choices. I WANT to see them try experimenting with things. This is "community" theater after all. I always advocate for my cast to try things even if it may contradict what I've initially told them. No one person could possibly know everything. Some of the greatest moments in cinema and stage history are a result of actor feedback. You never know if you don't try. Things of this manner, like ad libs and improv, are often associated with comedy, but this need not always be the case. These characters are far from one-sided. There are many different ways you could interpret them. I have an amazing sense of faith and confidence in my cast to make smart choices, and in the end, their interpretations of these characters will be all the better because of it.

**JaxPlays: At its heart, this story examines reasonable doubt, personal bias, group pressure and the responsibility that comes with making a life-altering decision. Which of these themes feels especially important to you in this production?**

**Turoff:** This show has so many strong and poignant themes that I could write a whole essay on it. For me personally though, one of the show's most important themes is critical thinking. One of Juror #8's first poignant lines, and one of my personal favorites is: "There were eleven votes for 'guilty.' It's not easy for me to raise my hand and send a boy off to die without talking about it first." You should never rush decisions, ESPECIALLY when it's life or death. You shouldn't always take the first answer just because it's the obvious one. A deeper look, and especially a new perspective, are crucial to understanding and overcoming adversity and obstacles. You should always be considering the "why?" instead of just doing blindly. Much of the case seems open and shut at first glance and even second glance. Throughout the story, Juror #8 shows them that there's more than meets the eye. In my opinion the evidence being used against the boy is incredibly circumstantial and full of inconsistencies. It says a lot that eleven of them, albeit some not intentionally, take all this evidence at first glance as foolproof, and try to send a boy to die. The phrase "Don't judge a book by its cover" may sound like what I'm getting at here, but I think a more appropriate phrase would be "Don't judge a book by its first chapter." It's important to go through life pondering the "why?" behind everything. Some of the greatest scientific breakthroughs came about because someone thought, "Why?" Human intellect and the capacity for abstract thought is one of humanity's greatest strengths. You see it all the time in the modern era of the internet, social media, and AI, where so many people are quick to make assumptions and rush judgements on things on which they know little. They never stop to ask the "why?" That quick rush of judgement is one of the notions this play challenges. It was relevant in 1957, and it is just as relevant now, if not more.

**JaxPlays: The audience watches the jurors debate the evidence and challenge one another's assumptions. How do you hope audiences will respond to the characters and reflect on their own beliefs as they watch the play unfold?**

**Turoff:** I'm hoping that theater goers are able to identify and relate to these characters just like I have. As much as I love the musical genre, most people don't just spontaneously break into song and dance at any given moment. This is a very grounded story with very grounded characters. These are normal people you'd see walking down the street on a typical day. People should be finding them relatable. I explained in the last question how important it is to consider the "why?" I believe that as the audience learns more about the case, and thinks on it along with the jurors, they'll start to think about the "why?" as well.

**JaxPlays: With so much of the action driven by dialogue, conflict and shifting power dynamics, what have been some of the biggest challenges, or most rewarding discoveries, in bringing this production to life?**

**Turoff:** It seems like such a simple story in theory: "Twelve people decide if someone is guilty or not." Seems rather basic, no? Well, to quote the late Paul Newman, "I prefer simple plots with complex characters rather than complex plots with simple characters." The story truly is just twelve people in a room for the whole show. Seems like a piece of cake to stage, like it probably wouldn't even need a director. But that's far from the truth. All of these characters are incredibly complex and multifaceted. While directing this show may seem easy at first, it definitely has had problems that aren't apparent at first glance, much like the evidence the jurors themselves examine. This isn't some flashy musical or slapstick comedy. You don't have things like humor, dancing, singing, etc to help keep the show moving. This show is reliant almost entirely on the performances of its stars. That is where its challenges lie. It can only work with a strong set of talented performers to accurately convey these characters, and my cast are all that and more.

**JaxPlays: Is there a particular moment, exchange or character dynamic that you find especially compelling, perhaps one that has taken on new meaning for you during rehearsals?**

**Turoff:** I won't go into too much detail as I don't want to spoil too much. There is a point later in the play where one of the more "antagonistic" (but sadly not unrealistic in today's world) jurors says some very nasty things. Seeing how all the jurors react to things in different ways is one of the best parts of this show in my opinion, and it especially shines in this moment.

**JaxPlays: What do you hope audiences take away from *Twelve Angry Jurors* when they leave the theatre?**

**Turoff:** As previously stated, strong examination and deep thought are crucial themes of this show. I believe that people will walk away from this show with a greater appreciation for abstract thought and critical thinking. We're hoping people can come to realize how dangerously quick humanity is to form strong opinions. The aim is to make the audience return from this show with a greater desire to ask "why?" I hope they can resume their lives ready to think about the reasons behind we do what we do. I want everyone to start looking at things harder and from different perspectives. Human thought should never be taken for granted.

**JaxPlays: Finally, what would you say to someone considering attending the production who may already know *Twelve Angry Men*, or someone experiencing this story for the very first time?**

**Turoff:** I would say that this story is as timeless as it gets. Its themes and greater implications were topical then, and they're topical now. This show challenges how you examine the world around you, and the ways in which we all interact with each other. It always has been, and always will be, one of my favorites. It's definitely worth a watch, especially if you're only familiar with the original movie. The difference in cast demographics compared to the all-male original makes it well worth the watch on its own. When adding on everything else, you'd be leaving this theater with a greater sense of intelligence, understanding, and patience.
