[Greenlight Theatre Company](/theatres/greenlight-theatre-company/) opens its production of the Tony Award-winning musical [*Avenue Q*](/productions/2026-avenue-q/) Friday, Oct. 2. Directed by [Rodney Holmes](/people/rodney-holmes/), the show features a cast of 14 actors performing alongside puppets to tell a story about navigating young adulthood, employment and relationships.<!--more-->

Ahead of opening night, JaxPlays spoke with Holmes about the art of puppet movement, his own history with the craft and the universal warmth behind the show's iconic humor.

**JaxPlays: What first drew you to *Avenue Q*, and what excited you most about the opportunity to direct it?**

**Rodney Holmes:** I have always loved *Avenue Q*. I first saw the tour back in 2011, and became immediately enamored with the music, the humor and the puppetry. Every time I have been fortunate enough to see a production since, I would sit in the audience and conceptualize my ideas of how I would want the show to be directed.

**JaxPlays: This is a Tony Award-winning musical that blends adult comedy, heart, music and puppetry in a pretty unique way. What do you think makes *Avenue Q* continue to resonate with audiences?**

**Holmes:** It's really a tale of growing up. Our main character is in that stage of his early twenties where he's trying to make sense of being thrown into "adulthood" but not really knowing what his next step is. There is a universal recognition that takes place between an audience and the story. Whether you have been through the struggle of the early-twenties confusion, or "real life" is creeping up on you, this show gives a sense that those experiences and feelings are more widely felt than you might imagine.

**JaxPlays: Puppetry is such a defining part of the show, and directing actors who are simultaneously performing and manipulating puppets is its own art form. What was that process like for you as a director?**

**Holmes:** Puppetry has always been a fascination of mine. Being one of the oldest forms of storytelling, there is something so raw and analog about the experience. Getting to impart some of my knowledge of puppetry to a group of mostly "puppet newbies" felt like being back in grade school playing with friends. There was so much exploration that happened. It's called a "play" for a reason, and with puppets, that playtime just becomes so much more enjoyable.

**JaxPlays: Did you have previous puppetry experience? If not, what did you have to learn or rethink as you approached the show?**

**Holmes:** I was fortunate enough to work for Disney Cruise Line, where I handled a variety of different puppets about 14 times a week. My puppet mentor, James Duncan, showed me the value of the little moments of puppetry that make the small, furry guys really come to life.

**JaxPlays: I know from my own experience working with puppets at Walt Disney World that puppetry is a skill. There's technique, physicality, focus and a whole different relationship between performer and character. How did you help your cast develop those skills?**

**Holmes:** We started right at the beginning of the process with what I called "Puppet Bootcamp." We stood in front of mirrors and I walked them through the basics of puppetry. I like to think about it like learning a language. We start with the "words" or mechanics. If I open and close my hand, what moves on the puppet? Then we move onto "vocabulary." When I move this arm one way, while tilting the head the other way, what emotion is the puppet expressing?

Then, when we understand the mechanics of the language, I let the actors write their own poetry. There's something incredible that happens when an actor makes a choice, as a human to, say, shake their head and sigh. When the puppet starts to move in tandem with the actors, that is where the real magic happens. It is symbiosis.

**JaxPlays: Did anyone involved in the production have formal puppetry training or experience? Were there workshops, coaching, rehearsal exercises or other experiential-learning opportunities that helped the cast find their puppets?**

**Holmes:** We had a few cast members who had some experience in puppetry. However, they were different styles of puppets from the ones we use in our show. It was like a professional skier trying surfing for the first time. Some of the mechanics translate, but different sets of muscles, physically and mentally, have to be trained.

**JaxPlays: How did the rehearsal process change once the puppets entered the room?**

**Holmes:** The first day we brought the puppets into the rehearsal room, I got a bit emotional. Watching the excitement from the cast, and the readiness to dive into one of my favorite art forms, brought me so much joy. This cast was an absolute dream. They were ready, from the second they slid the puppets on, to try anything and everything I threw at them.

**JaxPlays: Was there a moment when you felt the cast really began to "become" the characters?**

**Holmes:** We had a set of rehearsal puppets, which we used for the first few weeks of rehearsal, and a set of performance puppets which we used for the last few weeks. The day that the actors put their performance puppets on, there was a shift in the room. Before we even started rehearsal, I watched the actors walking around the room interacting with each other's puppets, and that's when it really clicked.

**JaxPlays: What are some of your favorite facets of the show, whether it's a particular character, musical number, relationship, theme or theatrical device?**

**Holmes:** Not to be cliche, but the final number of the show always brings the biggest smile to my face. The idea of all of the hardships in life being only "For Now" truly sums up the heart of the story. It's also the only time in the show that the entire cast is onstage at the same time. There's something wonderful about seeing a group of performers that you've grown to know and respect unified for a beautiful button to a show.

**JaxPlays: There's a lot of humor in *Avenue Q*, but underneath that humor are some pretty universal questions about growing up, finding your place, relationships, work and figuring out adulthood. What do you hope audiences take away from the show beyond the laughs?**

**Holmes:** Just like our main character, Princeton, you may not have everything figured out. Life isn't something that needs a rigid roadmap to it. Enjoy the ride, discover your own joys, and know that "everything in life is only for now."

**JaxPlays: What has surprised you most about directing this show, and has anything about the process or the material changed your own perspective?**

**Holmes:** Having seen this show numerous times, I always knew there was a lot of heart underneath all of the adult jokes and funny gags. Diving into it as a director, however, has given me an even deeper appreciation of the love and joy that this show brings.

**JaxPlays: How would you describe your artistic vision for this production?**

**Holmes:** I knew from the start that I wanted this show to be less spectacle driven. I wanted the actors' talents and the puppetry to really be a focus for this show. There was a balance that had to be found between the ridiculousness of actors walking around with bits of foam on their hands, and the instinctive choices of the actors themselves. Being an actor myself, I understand what it's like to want to make creative choices onstage. When I direct, I try to maintain my vision while also blending it with input from the actors. That little act of collaboration is, in my opinion, the real joy of community theatre.

**JaxPlays: And finally, if you could give audiences one little nudge before they come see the show, what would you tell them?**

**Holmes:** Be prepared to laugh, cry and feel like a kid again. With so many difficulties in the world right now, there is no better place to forget all of that than going to the theatre. Forget what you think you know about puppets, and come for two hours of humor, love and catharsis.
