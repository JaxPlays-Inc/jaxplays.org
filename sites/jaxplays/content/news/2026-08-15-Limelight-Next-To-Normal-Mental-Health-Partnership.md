When *[[production:2026 Next to Normal|Next to Normal]]* opens Aug. 13 at [[theatre:Limelight Theatre]], [[person:Jessica Alexander]] and [[person:Jimmy Alexander]] are not simply stepping into one of contemporary musical theatre's most demanding marriages. They are also carrying a production they chose, produced and shaped around a larger community conversation.<!--more-->

The Pulitzer Prize-winning musical, with music by Tom Kitt and book and lyrics by Brian Yorkey, follows Diana Goodman, a mother living with bipolar disorder, and the family trying to survive beside her. In Limelight's [[venue:Matuza Blackbox]], Jessica Alexander plays Diana opposite Jimmy Alexander as Dan Goodman. Blake Osner directs the production, and [[person:Rick Cirucci]] serves as mental health liaison.

For Jimmy Alexander, the project began with a long-held wish and a narrow window of artistic timing.

"I saw the show a lot of years ago and and literally every year since then — it's been like 15 years — I have felt like this is a show that I really just need to do right, and I need to share with people," he said. "It's a role I really wanted to play. It's a role that I really wanted Jess to play. And no one was doing it."

Eventually, he said, he approached Limelight and asked for something more than a role.

"The biggest part of it was that if I'm gonna do this show, I want to do it our way," Jimmy said. "I really wanted to do it right. And so I asked for full creative control. And they gave it to us. But with that I got a lot of responsibility and things that I wasn't necessarily used to."

He described the production as a partnership with Limelight rather than a standard Limelight-produced show.

"This isn't technically a Limelight show," Jimmy said. But, "they are supporting me immensely and I absolutely we could not have pulled it off without them."

That support includes the use of the Matuza Blackbox, front-of-house help and technical assistance. It also gives the Alexanders a small, intimate space for a musical about family life under emotional pressure.

Osner said the production is leaning into that closeness without becoming fully immersive.

"We don't involve the audience, but the set wraps around the audience," Osner said. "They will feel very included in the action visually from what they're experiencing."

That intimacy comes with a caution. The musical deals with mental illness, suicide-related material and family trauma, and Osner said patrons who know the subject matter may be difficult for them should think about where they sit.

"I think I would just suggest if anyone's coming and they know the subject matter may be triggering for them, they might want to choose not to sit on the front row," Osner said. "That might be a good idea. But it is a small space, which I think will work really well for the show in terms of feeling that intimacy."

The physical staging also includes a visible live band, split on both sides of the multi-level stage rather than tucked into a pit.

"They will not be sitting in like what you might think of a traditional orchestra pit for a traditional play," Osner said. "They won't be all together. So that will add an exciting element to the production. They will be visible to the audience as well."

For Osner, the production's musical challenge is not simply fitting musicians into the room. It is pushing singers past beauty when the story demands something rawer.

"There are times when you have to let the emotion come through," he said. "By the time you get emotional, the audience knows you can sing pretty. So stop worrying about singing pretty and just let the let the story come out."

Jessica Alexander said that approach has shaped her Diana.

"My goal is, as Blake was saying, not to sound pretty, not to look pretty, not to be like, oh, look at her," she said. "My goal is for people to feel like it's real, and so I have actually not been working on my technique of my singing at all. I've just been singing it like I speak it."

Diana is often reduced in conversation to her diagnosis, but Jessica said she is trying to keep the character's full humanity in view.

"I really connect with how she is very silly and she is very fun and she makes these sharp little comments the whole time," she said. "So yeah, it's not just this crazy bipolar lady. she's a real person and she has all these beautiful sides to her as well as her struggles."

She is also approaching Diana's relationship with Natalie differently than versions she has seen before.

"I think it's more impactful that I am close to her, but that she only gets tiny pieces of that," Jessica said. "She gets tiny pieces of me and then everything's all about Diana."

For Jimmy, Dan's role is not simply to stand sturdily beside Diana. It is to reveal the cost of being perceived as the family's rock.

"It's because he's been trying for so long to be everything for everybody and to be the rock for the family," Jimmy said. "But, you know, inside he's not always there, you know, and it takes its toll and it just gets to the point. Everybody breaks at some point, right?"

He said that Dan's story may reach men who do not arrive expecting to see themselves in a musical about mental illness.

"A lot of men do need help that they're afraid to ask for, you know?" Jimmy said. "Maybe particularly the ones that are coming, and they don't know what this show's about. Maybe we're able to sneak in under the radar because they don't have their defense up." Jimmy said. "Maybe we can make them see that they  don't ... I don't have to silently suffer with this."

The production's mental-health component began, Cirucci said, with a grassroots conversation. After hearing Jessica and Jimmy discuss the project, he approached Jimmy about connecting the show to local mental-health partners.

"I had always been a fan of the the musical and had always thought either next to normal or Dear Evan Hansen would be really interesting to try to build it into something bigger with the community," Cirucci said.

He proposed reaching out to organizations that could help audiences move from emotional response to practical next steps.

"Maybe we could reach out to some of those community partners to see if they'd like to have a broader dialogue about reducing mental health stigma," Cirucci said.

A key piece will be the Aug. 23 community conversation matinee, which Cirucci described as a talkback with community partners available before, during intermission and after the performance.

Cirucci said the idea is to start with a familiar theatre talkback but make it more purposeful, using the Aug. 23 event to "break down that fourth wall" and ask audience members more than just "Do you have questions?" but "Did something come up that you'd you'd like like to know more about?" Community partners will be available in the atrium before the show, during intermission and afterward for individual conversations that could carry the discussion further.

The production also plans to survey audience interest in future QPR suicide prevention training.

"It's trying to see if we can change the dialogue and see if those that do attend would be interested of even taking that next step of doing a two-hour suicide prevention training," Cirucci said. "That's not written in stone. It's more of another kind of thought bubble that Jimmy and I had."

Cirucci emphasized that the production is not trying to replace clinical expertise with theatre. Those conversations, he said, belong with people trained and ready to have them.

Instead, he hopes the show creates space for more personal conversations among audience members and gives people permission to ask what they may not know how to ask. The larger message, he said, is simple: "It's okay to have these questions."

Jessica Alexander said the show's power lies partly in reaching people who did not come looking for a mental-health conversation.

"Through art, movies and theater, you can reach people in ways where they're not necessarily looking for it," she said.

Osner put it similarly, arguing that theatre can create a kind of experiential understanding that a clinical explanation cannot.

"None of the characters in the show have any clinical experience, right? None of them have any training. They're living it," Osner said. "They're learning as they go. They're doing the best they can with what information they have."

And despite the weight of the material, he said, [[production: 2026 Next to Normal]] is not a musical without hope.

"Nnot all the questions are answered," Osner said. "I mean, not everybody's cured in the show, but it is a show of hope. There's hope at the end."

For the Alexanders, producing the musical has meant taking on far more than performance. Jessica called it part of a broader turn toward creating work together. Jimmy said the pressure has been intense, but he would not give up the creative control that made this version possible.

"I complain about it a lot, and it's a lot of pressure, but I don't think that I would have done it any differently," Jimmy said.

He added that the production is not primarily about making money. "I did not do this the money," Jimmy said. "My goal at the beginning was to break even. That's not gonna happen. I put way too much into this, but it's gonna be exactly what I want. And that's the important part. I'm doing this for the experience and for what we can give to people and give back to the community."

[[production: 2026 Next to Normal]] runs Aug. 13-30 at Limelight Theatre in St. Augustine. The production is recommended for audiences PG-13 because of mature themes. A Community Conversation Matinee is scheduled for Sunday, Aug. 23.
