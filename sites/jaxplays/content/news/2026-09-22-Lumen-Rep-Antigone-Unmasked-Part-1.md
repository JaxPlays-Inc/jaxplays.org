JACKSONVILLE, Fla. — Classical Greek tragedy is getting a contemporary update on the local stage as [[theatre:Lumen Repertory Theatre]] prepares to debut its latest original work, [[production:2026 Antigone: Unmasked]]. Written and directed by Lumen Rep artistic director [[person:Brian Niece]], the production marks the company's fifth original work in four years, solidifying its commitment to generating new theatrical literature.<!--more-->

JaxPlays' Maureen E. McCluskey connected with Niece to explore the artistic vision, physical language and creative process behind transforming Sophocles' timeless dilemma into an urgent work for the contemporary moment.

**Maureen McCluskey:** What drew you to this story, and what made you want to create your own adaptation of it for the stage?

**Brian Niece:** My doctoral work afforded me a deep dive into the full Theban Cycle, and I kept returning to *Antigone* as the place where the trilogy's central questions get asked most urgently: what do we owe the dead, what do we owe the living state, and what do we owe the truth. Sophocles' text still asks a question our own moment hasn't answered.

But the text itself, even in strong translation, tends to go stale on a contemporary stage. Though I enjoy reading translations and faithful adaptations, I really wouldn't want to watch them as an audience member. So I didn't want to adapt Sophocles. I wanted to write a new play that Sophocles made possible. This is our fifth original work in just four years, so by now it's less an experiment than a discipline we've built into who we are.

**McCluskey:** When you began adapting the piece, what did you feel was essential to preserve from the original, and where did you see opportunities to make it your own?

**Niece:** The moral architecture had to stay intact: a woman who will not let the state define what's true, a ruler who mistakes control for order, a chorus that watches and implicates. Everything else was open. *Antigone: Unmasked* takes some of Sophocles' characters and the bones of his plot and relocates them entirely, into a nation that resembles our own but sits in what might be an alternate reality. That gave me the freedom to build new stakes, new language and a theatrical vocabulary that belongs to now instead of ancient Athens.

**McCluskey:** What surprised you most during the adaptation process?

**Niece:** How much the play insisted on being about masks. I didn't set out to write a piece built around unmasking. The metaphor emerged from the characters themselves, from watching who in this story can afford to be seen and who can't. Who embraces their truth and who avoids it. That became the production's entire visual and staging language.

**McCluskey:** Did the story evolve in ways you didn't initially expect?

**Niece:** Yes. Antigone herself barely wears a mask in this production. She doesn't seek the protection of hiding. Creon does, and it's the Apprentice Company, part of our NEXTGEN initiative, who remove it. I didn't plan that when I started writing. It became the clearest way to say what the play is actually about.

**McCluskey:** What are some of the theatrical choices you're most excited for audiences to experience?

**Niece:** The production is built on three distinct playing spaces: a power platform, a witness ramp and a truth circle. Audiences will watch power, witness and truth physically occupy different territory all night, and watch what happens when characters cross between them. That physical geography extends into the bodies onstage. The NEXTGEN Apprentice Company shifts throughout the production, sometimes functioning as an extension of the Chorus, sometimes as agents of the State, and their physicality has to read differently in each mode.

They spent the summer training in Suzuki and Laban technique, and we've built directly on that foundation to develop a physical vocabulary specific to this production, distinct postures and movement qualities for witnessing versus enforcing. The sound world does a lot of work too, with some original incidental music alongside reimagined songs and compositions that form a distinct palette. And the masking and unmasking system tracks every character's point of irreversible truth. None of it is decoration. It's the argument of the play made visible.

**McCluskey:** How has working with this particular cast and creative team shaped your collective vision along the way?

**Niece:** This isn't a cast assembled for one show and then scattered. Kate, Maggie and Zonnetta have already appeared in Lumen Rep work. Noah has directed a previous Lumen Rep production. Eugene helped us launch the company in 2023. So there's a shared language in the room before rehearsal even starts, a trust that lets us move faster and dig deeper.

The NEXTGEN apprentices brought their own discipline to that trust. A summer of Suzuki and Laban work gave us a shared physical vocabulary to build from. So the movement language you'll see onstage grew out of collaboration, not just top-down direction. We're a team. That continuity is exactly what "Rep" in Lumen Rep is meant to mean. We're building toward a true year-round repertory company, an ensemble that returns and grows together production after production.

As Lumen Repertory Theatre continues to innovate, *Antigone: Unmasked* promises to deliver an immersive exploration of truth, power and identity. Through deliberate physical staging and ensemble trust, this dynamic reimagining breathes new life into ancient questions.
