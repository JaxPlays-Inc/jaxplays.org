Jacksonville playwright [[person:David Sacks]] brings a new romantic comedy to [[theatre:Players by the Sea]] this fall with [[production:2026 I Love You, Youre Perfect Now Sign Here|I Love You, You're Perfect... Now Sign Here]], a world-premiere play about love, family expectations and the paperwork that can complicate both.<!--more-->

The play follows Hannah, who comes from a wealthy, conservative Jewish family in Greenwich, Connecticut, and Michael, who comes from a blue-collar, third-generation Irish family from Brooklyn, as wedding plans force both families into the same room.

Sacks answered JaxPlays' questions about the play's long path to the stage, the challenge of writing family comedy and what he hopes audiences will carry out of the theatre.

**JaxPlays: What was the first spark for *I Love You, You're Perfect... Now Sign Here*? Did the story begin with a particular character, a real-life observation, a family conflict or simply the wonderfully ominous phrase "now sign here"?**

**David Sacks:** About 20 years ago, I read an article in The New York Times with the title, "I Love You, You're Perfect... Now Sign Here!" I thought it was an interesting title for a play, so I saved the article and forgot about it.

In 2022, at Players by the Sea, we produced a play I wrote called *The Opposite of Normal*. In that script were nine different scene locations. The set designer, Claire Cimino, did not let me hear the end of it. She said it was not a play, but really a movie. You can't write plays with so many different locations.

At that point, as I've never been one to shy away from a challenge, I decided I would write a play where the action all happens in the same space. I remembered that I wanted to write a comedy with the title, and so the idea for this play was born.

Actually, originally it was titled *See You Tomorrow?* because I worried about using a title so close to a play that already was pretty famous, *I Love You, You're Perfect, Now Change*, but when I found out a title can't be copyrighted, I went ahead with it.

In addition to all that, as if that weren't enough, I had already written two plays with the character Michael, one of which was produced at Players in 2006, called *Accidental Felon*. That was Michael as he was searching for the perfect woman. The other play, *Family Life* -- still unproduced -- is about Michael and Hannah after they've been married and have children. So the missing piece was obviously, to me anyway, Michael finding the woman of his dreams and getting married.

**JP: The title promises romance and paperwork in the same breath. What does “sign here” represent within the play -- the realities of marriage, the compromises relationships require, the influence of money and family, or something else?**

**DS:** "Now sign here" is a reference to prenuptial agreements. The play begins where Hannah's father wants her to get her fiance, Michael, to sign a prenup because Hannah's family is very wealthy. As it turns out, Michael's parents wind up asking him to get Hannah to sign a prenup to protect the fortune they just won in the lottery. The rest of the story is not directly related to the reference, "now sign here."

**Hannah and Michael come from sharply different family traditions: a wealthy, conservative Jewish family in Greenwich and a blue-collar, third-generation Irish family from Brooklyn. What interested you about bringing those two worlds together?**

I wanted to tell a story about differing cultures and how they might coexist; what the pitfalls might be in trying to bring such differing lifestyles together; and since I was so familiar with these particular groups, having grown up in New York and gone to school in New England, I felt qualified to do so. I've always been taught: Write what you know.

**JP: Stories about clashing families can easily rely on broad stereotypes. How did you work to make these families culturally recognizable while still allowing each character to feel specific, complicated and human?**

**DS:** Honestly, that's not how I write. That's especially not how I write comedy. I start with a story and then think about who might tell this particular story. Sometimes I do an outline, other times just a summary. Then I start writing, and the characters either come out on the screen organically. The story either gels or it doesn't.

At the risk of sounding cliche, the characters actually wind up presenting themselves to me. That's the first draft. The next 50 drafts are all about refining what came out organically. I'm not sure that answers the question, but there it is.

**JP: Hannah and Michael arrive in Greenwich expecting to plan a wedding, but the process forces them to confront much larger questions. What does planning a wedding reveal about a couple that ordinary dating may allow them to avoid?**

**DS:** Well, first, in this story, planning this wedding involves Hannah's mother, so I would have to say that ordinary dating allowed Michael and Hannah to avoid Ruth, Hannah's mother. And that would naturally go for Michael's parents as well. However, Sean and Fiona are far more laid back than Ruth and Abe.

Also, then there are the questions of where, who and how, which, when it comes to what to do on a date, can be far easier to navigate than a wedding, which might involve family ritual, distant relatives, old resentments, family feuds to avoid and so forth. I'm sure there are more differences, but those are the first that come to mind.

**JP: A prenuptial agreement can be perfectly practical, yet it can also sound like a vote of no confidence in a marriage. How does the prenup challenge Hannah and Michael's ideas about love, trust, independence and loyalty?**

**DS:** The challenge for Michael and Hannah comes from their parents. They don't need or want the prenups. Hannah is far more practical about it, however, especially when she learns that Michael's parents want her to sign one too. Michael is the one who finds it insulting and wholly unnecessary. He's more altruistic than Hannah and wants everyone to live in peace and love. He's the "Kumbaya" partner.

**The parents appear to create many of the obstacles facing the couple, but parents often interfere because they sincerely believe they are protecting their children. How did you approach writing them with enough empathy that they are more than simple antagonists?**

**DS:** I suppose you can say that, as for Hannah's parents, Abe comes out as a decent guy, but Ruth is the problem. The script provides evidence, sometimes lightly, sometimes in your face, that this is a complicated mother-daughter relationship that goes back years.

Hannah, being the only child of wealthy parents, rebels in her own way, not entirely, by moving to New York City -- a place her parents detest only because they had an "Out-of-Towners" experience -- and is working in an orphanage, presumably toward a Ph.D. in social work or psychology. That's never made clear, but didn't need to be. She falls in love with a non-Jew, but a soon-to-be doctor. So it's a mixed bag.

Michael's parents, on the other hand, especially Michael's father, are salt of the earth and have had some low points in life, so they have more of a live-and-let-live attitude, unless you threaten their children. Especially with Fiona: Don't mess with her kids.

**JP: Hannah's rabbi is present as she tries to distinguish her own wishes from those of her family. What role does faith play in the play, and what kind of guidance is Hannah truly seeking from the rabbi?**

**DS:** Faith plays no specific role in the story. The position of rabbi is revered in the Jewish religion as one who is sought after for counsel. Hannah, although she hasn't been inside a temple in 10 years at the time the story takes place, was raised to look up to her rabbi. So it is natural, when faced with very difficult decisions, that she reaches out to him for advice, as he is the one who is supposed to know everything. Of course, this particular rabbi may be past his prime.

**JP: Taylor and Rick spend much of the play advising Hannah and Michael while bickering through their own unresolved relationship. What can they recognize about their friends that they are initially unable -- or unwilling -- to recognize about themselves?**

**DS:** How attracted Michael and Hannah are to each other.

**How much of the play comes from experiences you have personally lived, witnessed or heard about? Are there any characters or conflicts that people close to you may recognize a little too easily?**

**DS:** No! That question always comes up, so I'm probably a bit sensitive about it. For once, however, I can honestly say that none of the characters are modeled after anyone close to me.

I've been on the sidelines of two mother-daughter relationships in my life -- my daughter and her mother, and my sister and our mother -- but there are really no parallels except to say that the mother-daughter relationship can be perilous at times, but there is, at bottom, the strongest bond imaginable.

**JP: How did the play change during the writing, reading or rehearsal process? Was there a character, relationship or plot development that became more important than you originally expected?**

**DS:** No particular plot point or relationship became more important. Comedy is hard enough. I wanted to make the story funny, but accessible. Interesting, but also poignant. But I have to say that the character of Rick, who derives his life from *Accidental Felon*, is my favorite character because he is everything I am not, nor ever was.

**JP: Beneath all the complications, what do you believe the play ultimately says about marriage and the merging of two families, and what do you hope audiences at Players by the Sea will be talking about on the way home?**

**DS:** My primary hope is that whoever sees this play will be talking about how much they laughed during the performance. Aside from that, maybe discussing which character they most identified with.
